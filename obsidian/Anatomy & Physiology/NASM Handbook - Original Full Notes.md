## Lesson 1: The Kinetic Chain

The **kinetic chain** is the human movement system — three interdependent systems working together:

|System|Role|
|---|---|
|**Nervous system**|Control centre — tells muscles when/how to contract|
|**Skeletal system**|Structural framework — bones as rigid levers|
|**Muscular system**|Movers and stabilisers — produces tension and movement|

**Core principle:** Dysfunction in ONE system affects the OTHER TWO. Efficient movement requires all three working in harmony.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│              THE KINETIC CHAIN                  │
│                                                 │
│   NERVOUS ──► MUSCULAR ──► SKELETAL             │
│  (controls)   (moves)      (framework)          │
│       ▲                       │                 │
│       └───────  feedback ─────┘                 │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## Lesson 2: The Skeletal System

### Functions of the Skeletal System

1. Provides shape and form
2. Supports and protects internal organs
3. Creates structure for movement (muscle attachment sites)
4. Produces blood (in marrow)
5. Stores minerals
6. Determines posture

### Two Divisions of the Skeleton

|Division|Bone count|Components|
|---|---|---|
|**Axial**|~80 bones|Skull, ribcage, vertebral column|
|**Appendicular**|~126 bones|Arms, legs, pelvic girdle|

**Total:** ~206 bones, ~177 involved in voluntary movement, forming 300+ joints.

### Five Types of Bones

|Type|Characteristics|Example|
|---|---|---|
|**Long**|Cylindrical shaft with widened ends|Femur|
|**Short**|Similar length and width, cube-like|Carpals (wrist)|
|**Flat**|Thin surfaces, protect organs|Sternum|
|**Irregular**|Unique shape and function|Vertebrae, sacrum|
|**Sesamoid**|Embedded in tendon over joint|Patella|

### Bone Surface Markings

**Depressions** (flattened/indented):

- **Fossa** — a common depression
- **Sulcus** — a groove allowing soft tissue passage

**Processes** (projections for attachments):

- **Condyles** — at bottom of femur / top of tibia (form knee joint)
- **Epicondyles** — on humerus (form elbow joint)
- **Tubercles** — on humerus (greater/lesser, attach shoulder muscles)
- **Trochanters** — on femur (attach hip muscles)
- **Spinous processes** — on vertebrae

### Bone Remodeling

Constant renewal through two processes:

- **Osteoclasts** — break DOWN old bone (resorption) — "Clasts Crush"
- **Osteoblasts** — BUILD new bone (formation) — "Blasts Build"

### Bone Mass Timeline

|Age range|What happens|
|---|---|
|Childhood–teens|Formation > removal (growing)|
|~20–30|Peak bone mass reached|
|30–50|Relatively stable|
|50+|Decline begins → osteoporosis risk|

### Exercise & Bone Health

**Weight-bearing = best for bones:**

- Resistance training
- Walking, jogging
- Body-weight squats, push-ups
- Climbing stairs
- Dancing

**Not optimal for bones (still good for muscles/cardio):**

- Swimming
- Cycling

**Why it matters:** Exercise, posture, activity level, and nutrition all affect bone growth, maturation, and functionality.

### The Vertebral Column

```
    C1-C7  Cervical (neck) — 7 vertebrae, support head
      ↓
    T1-T12 Thoracic (mid-back) — 12 vertebrae, anchor ribs
      ↓
    L1-L5  Lumbar (lower back) — 5 vertebrae, largest, bear most weight
      ↓
    Sacrum — 5 fused vertebrae, triangular
      ↓
    Coccyx — 3-5 fused bones, tailbone
```

**Key structures:**

- **Intervertebral discs** — fibrous cartilage, shock absorbers, allow spine movement
- **Neutral spine** — optimal S-curve position where vertebrae are under least load

### Joints — Classification

**Two main types:**

|Type|Movement|Example|
|---|---|---|
|**Non-synovial**|Little to no movement|Skull sutures|
|**Synovial**|Free movement (~80% of joints)|Hip, knee, shoulder|

### Six Types of Synovial Joints

| Joint             | Movement                                      | Example                                           |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| **Ball & socket** | All three directions (most mobile)            | Hip, shoulder                                     |
| **Hinge**         | One direction like a door                     | Elbow, knee, toes                                 |
| **Pivot**         | Rotation around axis                          | Atlantoaxial (base of skull), proximal radioulnar |
| **Gliding/plane** | Non-axial, back/forth or side/side (simplest) | Carpals of wrist, vertebrae, ankle                |
| **Condyloid**     | Predominantly one direction, minimal others   | Fingers, jaw                                      |
| **Saddle**        | Back-forth + side-side, limited rotation      | Carpometacarpal of thumb                          |

### Arthrokinematics vs Osteokinematics

|Type|Meaning|
|---|---|
|**Osteokinematic**|Movements we can SEE (e.g. elbow bending)|
|**Arthrokinematic**|Movements INSIDE the joint (roll, slide, spin)|

**Three arthrokinematic movement types:**

- **Roll** — like a tyre on road (femur on tibia in squat)
- **Slide** — like a skid (tibia on femur in knee extension)
- **Spin** — like twisting a jar lid (forearm pronation/supination)

### Synovial Joint Components

- **Joint capsule** — loosely holds bones together
- **Ligaments** — static/dynamic stability
- **Cartilage** — pads ends of articulating bones
- **Synovial membrane** — secretes synovial fluid
- **Synovial fluid** — lubricates joint surfaces (like engine oil), nourishes cartilage

### Ligaments

- Connect **BONE to BONE**
- Primary protein: **collagen** (tensile strength)
- Secondary protein: **elastin** (flexibility/elastic recoil)
- **Poor vascularity** (blood supply) → slow to heal
- Provide joint stability + sensory input to nervous system (proprioception)

**Example:** ACL has very little elastin, mostly collagen — great for resisting strong forces, excellent knee stabiliser.

### Joint Function & Kinetic Chain

**Key concept:** All joints are linked. Movement of one directly affects others.

_Demonstration:_ Roll feet inward/outward → knees and hips move too. This is why if one joint isn't working correctly, it affects all joints it works with.

## Lesson 3: The Muscular System

### Three Types of Muscle

|Type|Voluntary?|Location|Function|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Skeletal**|Voluntary|Attached to bones|Movement, posture|
|**Cardiac**|Involuntary|Heart|Pump blood|
|**Smooth**|Involuntary|Organs, digestive tract|Organ function|

### Functions of the Muscular System

1. Produce movement
2. Provide posture
3. Stabilise the body
4. Generate heat (homeostasis)
5. Support skeletal system

### Muscle Structure (Russian Nesting Dolls)

```
Muscle (whole)
  └── Epimysium (deep fascia, wraps whole muscle)
      └── Fascicles (bundles of fibres)
          └── Perimysium (wraps each fascicle)
              └── Muscle fibres (cells)
                  └── Sarcolemma (plasma membrane of fibre)
                      └── Sarcoplasm (contains glycogen, fats, minerals, myoglobin, mitochondria)
                          └── Myofibrils
                              └── Sarcomeres ← contraction happens here
                                  └── Myofilaments: Actin (thin) + Myosin (thick)
```

**Key structural terms:**

- **Sarcomere** = functional unit of muscle contraction (where it actually happens)
- **Z-line** = boundary between sarcomeres

### Connective Tissues of Muscle

- Extend length of muscle → form **tendon** at ends
- Transmit forces from muscle to bone
- Allow muscles to work as functional groups

### Tendons vs Ligaments (easy confusion point)

||Tendons|Ligaments|
|---|---|---|
|Connect|**Muscle to bone**|**Bone to bone**|
|Blood supply|Poor|Poor|
|Healing|Slow|Slow|

### Neural Activation — How Muscles Contract

**Key structures:**

- **Motor neuron** — nerve cell controlling muscle
- **Motor unit** = one motor neuron + ALL muscle fibres it innervates
- **Neuromuscular junction** — where nerve meets muscle
- **Synapse** — the small gap at the junction

### Excitation-Contraction Coupling (full process)

```
1. Nerve impulse begins in CNS
2. Travels down motor neuron (facilitated by sodium + potassium electrolytes)
3. Reaches neuromuscular junction
4. Acetylcholine released into synapse (neurotransmitter)
5. Impulse crosses into muscle
6. Sarcoplasmic reticulum releases CALCIUM
7. Calcium triggers myosin heads to bind to actin
8. Myosin heads pull actin toward sarcomere centre (POWER STROKE)
9. Filaments slide past each other → sarcomere shortens → muscle contracts
10. ATP provides energy throughout
11. Nerve stops firing → muscle relaxes, returns to resting length
```

**This is the Sliding Filament Theory** — actin and myosin sliding past each other produces contraction.

### Motor Units & the All-or-Nothing Principle

- Motor units either fire COMPLETELY or NOT AT ALL
- Force depends on:
    1. Which units fire (large vs small)
    2. How many fire at once

### Muscle Fibre Types

|Feature|Type I (Slow twitch)|Type II (Fast twitch)|
|---|---|---|
|Speed|Slow|Fast|
|Fatigue|Resistant|Fatigues quickly|
|Force|Smaller|Greater|
|Size|Smaller|Larger|
|Motor units|Small|Large|
|Primary use|Endurance, posture, stability|Power, sprinting, jumping|
|Capillaries|Many|Few|
|Mitochondria|Many|Few|
|Myoglobin|High → RED fibres|Low → WHITE fibres|
|Oxidative capacity|High|Low|

**Analogy:** Myoglobin is like hemoglobin (the red pigment in blood) — that's why slow-twitch fibres are red.

**All muscles contain BOTH types.** Proportions vary by muscle function and genetics. Bigger muscles tend to have more Type II.

### Muscle Roles

|Role|Function|Example (bicep curl)|
|---|---|---|
|**Agonist** (prime mover)|Does the work|Biceps|
|**Antagonist**|Opposes prime mover|Triceps|
|**Synergist**|Assists prime mover|Brachialis|
|**Stabiliser**|Contracts isometrically to prevent unwanted movement|Rotator cuff, TVA|

**Important:** A muscle's role changes based on the movement. The infraspinatus can be:

- Agonist for shoulder external rotation
- Synergist for overhead reach
- Stabiliser for other shoulder motions

### Ageing & Muscle

|Age|What happens|
|---|---|
|20–30|Peak mass, power, strength|
|30–50|Minimal changes|
|50+|Lose 1–2% muscle mass per year|

**Best defence:** Resistance training combats decline for aging adults.

## Lesson 4: The Nervous System (Introduction)

### Three Primary Functions

|Function|What it does|
|---|---|
|**Sensory**|Detects changes internally + externally|
|**Integrative**|Analyses + interprets sensory info, makes decisions|
|**Motor**|Body's response via neuromuscular system|

### Two Divisions

```
NERVOUS SYSTEM
│
├── CENTRAL NS (CNS) — interprets information
│   ├── Brain
│   └── Spinal cord
│
└── PERIPHERAL NS (PNS) — connects CNS to body
    ├── 12 cranial nerves
    ├── 31 pairs of spinal nerves
    └── Sensory receptors
```

### Neuron Anatomy

- **Cell body (soma)** — contains nucleus + organelles, controls cell functions
- **Axon** — cylindrical projection, sends signals OUT to other neurons/muscles/organs
- **Dendrites** — gather info from other structures, transmit INTO neuron

### Key Electrolytes for Nerve Function

Sodium, potassium, magnesium, water — must be adequate for proper nerve impulse transmission.

### Pathways

|Pathway|Direction|Role|
|---|---|---|
|**Afferent** (sensory)|PNS → CNS (up to brain)|Sensory info|
|**Efferent** (motor)|CNS → PNS (down to body)|Motor commands|
|**Interneurons**|Within CNS only|Between afferent/efferent|

### Sensory Receptor Types

|Type|Responds to|Fitness relevance|
|---|---|---|
|**Mechanoreceptors**|Touch, pressure, motion|★ Most important|
|**Nociceptors**|Pain|—|
|**Chemoreceptors**|Smell, taste|—|
|**Photoreceptors**|Light|—|

## Lessons 1-2: Nervous System in Depth

### PNS Further Subdivisions

```
Peripheral Nervous System
│
├── SOMATIC NS → voluntary movement (skeletal muscles)
│
└── AUTONOMIC NS → involuntary (organs, glands)
    │
    ├── SYMPATHETIC → "Fight or Flight"
    │   ↑ HR, ↑ breathing, muscles ready, adrenaline released
    │   (active during exercise)
    │
    └── PARASYMPATHETIC → "Rest and Digest"
        ↓ HR, ↓ breathing, recovery, digestion
```

**Fitness relevance:**

- Chronic stress → overactive sympathetic → can't rest/recover
- Healthy clients need balance between the two
- Breathing exercises and slow cardio shift toward parasympathetic

### Mechanoreceptors — Three Types Critical for Movement

|Receptor|Location|Responds to|Purpose|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Muscle spindles**|Within muscle, parallel to fibres|Change in muscle LENGTH + rate of change|Stretch reflex — prevents overstretching (response in 1-2 ms)|
|**Golgi Tendon Organs (GTO)**|At muscle-tendon insertion|Change in muscle TENSION + rate of change|Triggers muscle relaxation to prevent injury|
|**Joint receptors**|In/around joint capsule|Pressure, acceleration, deceleration|Signals extreme joint positions|

**Both spindles and GTOs are critical for flexibility training.**

**Pilates application:** Held stretches activate GTOs → muscle relaxes deeper. This is the physiological reason slow, held stretches work.

### Proprioception

The body's natural ability to sense its general orientation and the relative position of all its parts — without looking.

**Training proprioception improves:**

- Balance
- Coordination
- Posture
- Injury prevention
- Adapting to surroundings without conscious thought

### Motor Skill Learning — Three Stages

Critical for teaching Pilates clients new exercises:

|Stage|What's happening|How to teach|
|---|---|---|
|**1. Cognitive**|Client is new to skill, inconsistent performance|Simple instructions, break skill into small steps|
|**2. Associative**|Client is getting it, understanding the skill|Refine through practice and regular feedback|
|**3. Autonomous**|Skill mastered, performed automatically|Challenge with variations, add complexity|

**Background science:** Motor skill learning relates to brain development through **neuroplasticity** (brain's ability to change) and **neurocircuitry** (neural pathway formation).

### Nervous System Development

- Adolescent brain develops from age 10 to 25
- Influenced by heredity, environment, medications
- CNS continues to acquire new skills throughout life ("old dogs can learn new tricks")
- Physical and cognitive decline with aging — **physical fitness is one of the best counters**

## Lesson 3 (Part 1): Cardiovascular System

### Overview

The **cardiorespiratory system** = cardiovascular + respiratory systems working together to:

- Deliver O₂ and nutrients to tissues
- Remove CO₂ and waste products
- Ensure optimal cellular function

### The Heart

- Muscular pump, involuntary cardiac muscle
- Located obliquely in thoracic cavity, slightly left of sternum
- Size of adult fist, ~300g (10 oz)
- Similar structure to skeletal muscle — has actin/myosin myofibrils that cross-bridge

### Four Chambers, Two Pumps

```
         LUNGS
          ▲ ▼
    ┌──────────────┐
    │  L.A │ R.A   │  ← ATRIA (smaller, reservoirs, on top)
    │──────┼───────│
    │  L.V │ R.V   │  ← VENTRICLES (larger, pumps, on bottom)
    └──────────────┘
       ▼      ▲
     BODY   BODY
    (oxy)  (deoxy)
```

|Chamber|Role|
|---|---|
|**Right atrium**|Receives deoxygenated blood FROM body (via vena cavae)|
|**Right ventricle**|Pumps deoxygenated blood TO lungs (thin walls, low pressure — short distance)|
|**Left atrium**|Receives oxygenated blood FROM lungs|
|**Left ventricle**|Pumps oxygenated blood TO entire body (THICK walls, high pressure — long distance)|

**Key point:** The left ventricle is the primary site for cardiac adaptations from training (it works hardest).

### Complete Blood Flow Path

```
BODY (deoxygenated)
  ↓
Superior Vena Cava (upper body) + Inferior Vena Cava (lower body)
  ↓
RIGHT ATRIUM
  ↓ (tricuspid valve — 3 flaps)
RIGHT VENTRICLE
  ↓ (pulmonary artery)
LUNGS ← PULMONARY CIRCULATION
  ↓ (drops CO₂, picks up O₂)
  ↓ (pulmonary veins)
LEFT ATRIUM
  ↓ (mitral valve)
LEFT VENTRICLE
  ↓ (aorta)
BODY (oxygenated) → SYSTEMIC CIRCULATION
```

### One-Way Valves

Each chamber separated by one-way valves to prevent backflow:

- **Tricuspid** — between right atrium and right ventricle
- **Mitral** — between left atrium and left ventricle

### Heart's Electrical Conduction System

```
SA Node (right atrium) — the "PACEMAKER"
  ↓ (internodal pathways)
AV Node (between atria and ventricles)
  ↓ (delays impulse so ventricles fill with blood)
Purkinje fibres → myocardial cells contract
  ↓
Normal sinus rhythm
```

### Key Heart Measurements

|Term|Definition|Normal|
|---|---|---|
|**Heart rate (HR)**|Beats per minute|60–100 bpm at rest|
|**Bradycardia**|Low HR|Below 60 bpm|
|**Tachycardia**|High HR|Above 100 bpm|
|**Stroke volume (SV)**|Blood pumped per beat|~75–80 ml|
|**End-diastolic volume**|Blood filling ventricle BEFORE contraction|—|
|**End-systolic volume**|Blood remaining AFTER contraction|—|
|**SV calculation**|End-diastolic minus end-systolic|—|
|**Cardiac output (Q)**|HR × SV|~5 L/min at rest|

**Trained athletes** often have lower resting HR — the heart pumps more efficiently.

### Blood — Three Mechanisms

Blood = ~8% of body weight, ~5 litres in average adult.

**Components:**

- **Plasma** — watery liquid with nutrients (glucose, lipids, proteins, hormones, clotting agents)
- **Red blood cells** — carry oxygen from lungs throughout body
- **White blood cells** — fight infection
- **Platelets** — clotting + healing (contain growth factors)

**Three support mechanisms:**

|Mechanism|Functions|
|---|---|
|**Transport**|O₂, nutrients, waste, hormones, heat|
|**Regulation**|Body temperature, pH (acid balance — via plasma), water content|
|**Protection**|Clotting, immune cells (fight disease)|

### Blood Vessels

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────┐
│   Heart → Aorta → Arteries → Arterioles  │
│                                      ↓   │
│          CAPILLARIES (exchange site)     │
│                                      ↓   │
│   Heart ← Veins ← Venules ← Capillaries  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

|Vessel|Function|
|---|---|
|**Aorta**|Largest artery — carries blood from heart|
|**Arteries**|Carry blood AWAY from heart|
|**Arterioles**|Smaller branches|
|**Capillaries**|Smallest vessels — exchange O₂, CO₂, nutrients, waste|
|**Venules**|Collect blood from capillaries|
|**Veins**|Carry blood BACK to heart (have one-way valves)|

**Major arteries (branches of aorta):**

- **Carotid** — brain
- **Subclavian** — arms
- **Mesenteric** — digestive system
- **Renal** — kidneys
- **Iliac** — pelvic/reproductive organs

**Capillary formation:**

- **Vasculogenesis** — formation of NEW capillaries
- **Angiogenesis** — new capillaries from EXISTING vessels
- Growth factors from platelets aid both

### Blood Pressure

- Measurement of blood circulating against artery walls
- Based on: cardiac output + peripheral resistance
- Arteries constricted → peripheral resistance ↑
- Arteries dilated → peripheral resistance ↓

|Pressure|When it occurs|Normal|
|---|---|---|
|**Systolic**|Heart contracts|<120 mmHg|
|**Diastolic**|Heart relaxes (filling with blood)|<80 mmHg|

**Hypertension** = chronically elevated BP. Multiple stages.

## Lesson 3 (Part 2): Respiratory System

### Overview

Also called the **pulmonary system**. Includes:

- Airways
- Lungs
- Respiratory muscles

**Primary role:** Ensure cellular function by:

- Bringing O₂ in from environment
- Transferring O₂ to bloodstream
- Removing CO₂ from blood
- Expelling CO₂ out of body

**Bonus function:** Filter 7,000–9,000 litres of air daily — removes foreign particles and germs.

### Breathing (Ventilation)

**Two phases:**

|Phase|Mechanism|Normal resting state|
|---|---|---|
|**Inspiration** (inhalation)|Active — muscles contract, thoracic cavity expands, intrapulmonary pressure drops below atmospheric, air drawn in|Uses diaphragm + external intercostals|
|**Expiration** (exhalation)|Passive at rest — inspiratory muscles relax. Active in heavy breathing — expiratory muscles compress cavity|—|

### Respiratory Muscles

**Primary (normal breathing):**

- Diaphragm
- External intercostals (between ribs)

**Secondary (heavy breathing):**

- Scalenes (neck)
- Sternocleidomastoid (neck)
- Pectoralis minor
- Abdominal muscles (active expiration)

### Structures of the Respiratory Pump

Sternum, ribs, vertebrae, diaphragm, intercostals, neck muscles, pec minor, abdominals — all work in harmony.

### Respiratory Rates

|State|Rate|
|---|---|
|Normal rest|12–16 breaths/min|
|**Tachypnea**|>24 breaths/min (too fast)|
|**Bradypnea**|<8 breaths/min (too slow)|

### Respiratory Passageways — Two Categories

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  CONDUCTING AIRWAYS                                │
│  (no gas exchange — just channel/prepare air)      │
│                                                    │
│  Nose/mouth → Pharynx → Larynx → Trachea →         │
│  Bronchi → Bronchioles                             │
│                                                    │
│  Purpose: Filter, humidify, warm/cool air          │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  RESPIRATORY AIRWAYS                               │
│  (gas exchange happens here)                       │
│                                                    │
│  At end of bronchioles → ALVEOLI (alveolar sacs)   │
│                                                    │
│  Purpose: Gas exchange via DIFFUSION               │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Gas Exchange — The Full Process

```
1. Air inhaled through nose/mouth
2. Travels through trachea, bronchi, bronchioles
3. Reaches alveolar sacs
4. Simultaneously: deoxygenated blood pumped from right ventricle to lungs
5. Blood enters pulmonary capillaries surrounding alveoli
6. Oxygen DIFFUSES from alveoli across capillary membranes into blood
7. Oxygenated blood returns to left atrium via pulmonary veins
8. Pumped to body from left ventricle
9. Cells use O₂, produce CO₂
10. CO₂ carried back to lungs, diffuses into alveoli
11. Exhaled out
```

**Beautiful exchange:** As O₂ comes in, CO₂ goes out. They trade places in tissues, blood, and lungs.

### Respiratory Pump & Venous Return

- During inspiration, intrathoracic pressure DROPS
- This drops pressure in right atrium
- Helps circulate blood BACK to heart
- This is why proper breathing improves circulation

### Oxygen Uptake

The body's use of oxygen. Depends on:

1. Respiratory system's ability to COLLECT oxygen
2. Cardiovascular system's ability to ABSORB and TRANSPORT
3. Tissues/cells' ability to USE oxygen

**Critical fact for weight loss clients:** Fat cannot be burned without oxygen. As a carbon-based life form, the only way mass can be physically removed (aside from surgery) is through CO₂ exhaled with every breath.

### Abnormal Breathing Patterns — Warning Signs

**Symptoms of rapid/shallow breathing:**

- Dizziness
- Hyperventilation
- Visible contraction of secondary respiratory muscles (neck)
- Altered CO₂/O₂ blood content
- Anxiety feelings

**Consequences of improper breathing:**

- Restricted spine and ribcage movement
- Joint stiffness
- Headaches
- Fatigue
- Poor sleep
- Difficulty with exercise
- Poor circulation

**Scope of practice reminder:** If a client shows abnormal breathing patterns, refer to medical professional. Fitness professionals CAN teach correct breathing to enhance performance, health, wellness.

### Exercise Adaptations

|System|Adaptation with training|
|---|---|
|**Cardiovascular**|↑ Cardiac output (from stroke volume + HR), ↓ resting HR, new capillaries (capillarisation)|
|**Respiratory**|↑ Respiratory muscle strength, better O₂ delivery, better CO₂ removal|
|**Muscular**|↑ Size, strength, endurance. Better motor unit synchronisation and rate coding (early strength gains are neural!)|

**Early strength gains = nervous system adaptations**, not muscle size. The body learns to recruit the right muscle at the right time with better motor unit synchronisation.

# Week 3 — Bioenergetics & Energy Systems

---

## week: 3 topics: [bioenergetics, ATP, phosphagen, glycolysis, oxidative, VO2max, lactate-threshold, programming]

## The Core Concept

**Bioenergetics** = study of how chemical energy (food) is converted into mechanical energy (movement/work).

Chemical bonds broken → energy released → muscle contractions.

**ATP (adenosine triphosphate)** = the body's universal energy currency.

**Problem:** Cells store ATP in limited quantities. The body must CONTINUOUSLY produce more via three bioenergetic pathways.

---

## The Bioenergetic Continuum

**Analogy:** Three gears of a car

- **1st gear (ATP-PCr)** — explosive burst off the line
- **2nd gear (Glycolysis)** — moderate speed with noise
- **3rd gear (Oxidative)** — cruise control for long haul

**Key principle:** All three are ALWAYS running — one just dominates based on intensity and duration.

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│        ENERGY SYSTEM CONTINUUM                               │
│                                                              │
│  INTENSITY ─────────────────────────────► DURATION           │
│                                                              │
│  ATP-PCr     ──►  Glycolysis    ──►  Oxidative               │
│  (0-10 sec)      (30-60 sec)         (2+ min)                │
│  ANAEROBIC       ANAEROBIC           AEROBIC                 │
│  90-100% int.    80-90% int.         Low-moderate            │
│                                                              │
│  Fuel:           Fuel:                Fuel:                  │
│  Phosphocreatine Glucose              Carbs, fats, protein   │
│                                                              │
│  2 ATP gain:     2 ATP/glucose        36-38 ATP/glucose      │
│  N/A (stored)                                                │
│                                                              │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

---

## System 1: ATP-PCr (Phosphagen System)

- Also called the **phosphagen system**
- **ATP + PCr (phosphocreatine)** both stored in muscle cells
- **Anaerobic** — no oxygen needed
- Provides IMMEDIATE energy

**Key facts:**

- Energy for first ~10 seconds of ALL activity
- Activated at onset of any activity (fastest production)
- Used for high-intensity, short-duration bouts
- Examples: sprinting, heavy weightlifting, vertical jumps
- Think: 90–100% intensity

**Why it's limited:** Stored PCr runs out quickly. If activity continues, must reduce intensity and transition to glycolysis.

---

## System 2: Glycolysis

- **Anaerobic** — no oxygen
- Breaks down GLUCOSE (sugar/carbs) to produce ATP
- Each glucose molecule → **2 ATP** (through anaerobic glycolysis)

**Two sub-types:**

|Type|Oxygen present?|Duration|Result|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Fast glycolysis**|No (anaerobic)|Up to 1-2 min|Lactic acid build-up|
|**Slow glycolysis**|Yes (aerobic)|Longer|No lactic acid (pyruvate → oxidative)|

### Lactic Acid

- By-product of anaerobic glycolysis (pyruvate if can't be utilized fast enough → lactic acid)
- Build-up increases muscle cell acidity
- Interferes with muscle contractions → **performance decreases**

**Duration:** Approximately 30–60 seconds

**Examples:**

- Typical 8–12 rep resistance training set
- 400m run
- Moderate-to-high intensity circuits

**Intensity level:** ~80–90%

---

## System 3: Oxidative System

- **Aerobic** — requires oxygen
- Relies primarily on carbohydrates and fats (can use protein)
- Oxygen supplied through respiration — takes time to ramp up

**Process:**

- Similar to anaerobic glycolysis BUT with oxygen
- Pyruvate doesn't convert to lactic acid — becomes substrate for ATP production
- Uses **Krebs cycle + electron transport chain** in mitochondria

**ATP yield:**

- 36 ATP per glucose molecule
- Can be up to 38 ATP depending on substrates/pathways
- **WAY more efficient than glycolysis**

**Duration:** Predominant in activities >2 minutes. Involvement increases over 30 seconds.

**Examples:**

- Long jog, bike ride
- Walking
- Daily activities (working, standing, sitting)
- **Primary energy system for majority of daily life**

**Fuel at low intensity** = primarily fat.

---

## Key Principle: Intensity × Duration

**Inverse relationship:**

- Intensity ↑ = Duration ↓
- Intensity ↓ = Duration ↑

Think of it as a trade-off:

- Explosive jump = very short
- 400m run = moderate duration
- Long hike = much longer but easier

---

## Programming by Energy System

|Client goal|Target system|Programming approach|
|---|---|---|
|Power/explosive strength|ATP-PCr|Short max effort, LONG rest (2–5 min) to replenish PCr|
|Muscular endurance/hypertrophy|Glycolysis|8–12 reps, 30–90 sec rest, circuit training|
|Cardiovascular/fat loss|Oxidative|Long duration, low-moderate intensity|

### Work-to-Rest Ratios

Understanding energy systems = understanding how to structure rest:

- High intensity → needs longer rest
- Moderate intensity → short rest keeps glycolysis firing
- Low intensity → minimal rest for oxidative adaptation

### Sagittal Plane Movements (Flexion/Extension)

Top of body → down:

|Joint|Flexion|Extension|
|---|---|---|
|**Cervical**|Chin toward chest|Chin away from chest|
|**Spinal**|Forward bend|Backbend|
|**Shoulder**|Arm raises forward|Arm lowers/extends back|
|**Elbow**|Bending (bicep curl up)|Straightening (bicep curl down)|
|**Wrist**|Palm toward forearm|Backhand away|
|**Hip**|Thigh toward trunk (two ways: femur-on-pelvis OR pelvis-on-femur/hip-hinge)|Femur away from hip|
|**Knee**|Heel toward back of thigh|Heel away|
|**Ankle**|**Dorsiflexion** (toes up, toward shin)|**Plantar flexion** (toes down, pointing)|

**Hyperextension:** Extension BEYOND normal limit → possible injury.

### Frontal Plane Movements

**Abduction** = away from midline **Adduction** = toward midline

|Joint|Abduction|Adduction|
|---|---|---|
|**Shoulder**|Arm raises out to side|Arm lowers toward body|
|**Hip**|Leg raises out to side|Leg returns to neutral|

**Spine:** Lateral flexion (side bend) **Cervical:** Lateral flexion (ear to shoulder)

**Foot-specific:**

- **Eversion** — arch collapses inward (weight on inside of foot)
- **Inversion** — weight on outside edge of foot

### Transverse Plane Movements

**Rotational movements:**

|Joint|Movement|
|---|---|
|**Cervical**|Rotation left/right (shaking head "no")|
|**Spinal**|Trunk rotation|
|**Shoulder**|Internal rotation (humerus rotates inward) / External rotation (outward)|
|**Forearm**|**Radioulnar supination** (palm up — "soup") / **Pronation** (palm down — "prone to spill")|
|**Hip**|Internal rotation (toes toward midline) / External rotation (toes away)|

**Horizontal abduction/adduction** — at shoulder/hip when joint is flexed 90°:

- Horizontal abduction = arm moves from front to side
- Horizontal adduction = arm moves from side to front
- Examples: chest fly, chest press — start in horizontal abduction, move to horizontal adduction

### Foot — Multiplanar Movement

The foot rarely performs single-plane movements in isolation.

**Foot pronation** = multiplanar combination:

- Eversion (frontal)
- Dorsiflexion (sagittal)
- Forefoot abduction (transverse)

**Foot supination** = multiplanar combination:

- Inversion (frontal)
- Plantar flexion (sagittal)
- Forefoot adduction (transverse)

**Walking/running:** Foot moves between pronation (force reduction) and supination (force production).

### Scapular Movements (Translations)

Scapular movements are called **translations** (not rotations around fixed axis).

|Movement|Description|
|---|---|
|**Retraction**|Shoulder blades together|
|**Protraction**|Shoulder blades apart|
|**Elevation**|Upward (toward ears)|
|**Depression**|Downward|

### Force Couple Relationship

Multiple muscles acting on same joint to produce ONE coordinated movement — even if forces are in different directions.

**Car steering analogy:** Left hand pulls down, right hand pushes up → car turns left.

**Body example — upward rotation of scapula (overhead reach):**

- Serratus anterior
- Upper trapezius
- Lower trapezius

All three pull scapula in different directions, but coordinated = smooth upward movement.

## Lesson 2: Muscle Actions, Levers, Open/Closed Chain

### Muscle Action Spectrum

**Bicep curl illustrates all three:**

|Action|What happens|Force vs Load|Curl phase|Synonym|Example|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|**Concentric**|Muscle SHORTENS|Force > load|Lifting up|Acceleration|Lifting weight|
|**Isometric**|Muscle maintains LENGTH|Force = load|Holding top|Stabilisation|Pause during lift|
|**Eccentric**|Muscle LENGTHENS (returning to resting length, not stretching)|Force < load|Lowering down|Deceleration|Lowering the weight ("negative")|

**Key programming principle:** Most training overemphasises CONCENTRIC. Train all three:

- Maximises strength potential
- Maintains joint range of motion
- Prevents injury

## Muscle Imbalances & Length-Tension Relationship

### Ideal Muscle Balance

- Both sides of joint have optimal length
- **Ideal length-tension relationship** = optimal tension for optimal force

### Imbalance Pattern

```
Short/overactive muscle ← → Long/underactive muscle
(tight, too much tone)    (lengthened, weak, inhibited)
```

**Short/overactive** = pulls bone toward it (tension bias) **Long/underactive** = allows the other muscle to pull bone out of position

**Result:** Joint in suboptimal position → increased stress → risk of injury.

### Regional Interdependence (the Domino Effect)

Joints depend on other joints. Dysfunction cascades.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────┐

│                                     │

│           ▼                         │

│           ▼                         │

│           ▼                         │
│  Shoulder compensation              │
│           ▼                         │
│  Upper body misalignment            │
│                                     │
└─────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Key insight:** One imbalance anywhere creates a chain reaction throughout the body. This is why Pilates and fitness professionals assess WHOLE-BODY posture — not just the problem area.

# Final Assessment — Squat Analysis

## Feet and Ankles

**Muscles involved:** Tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius, soleus, peroneals.

**Function:** The feet and ankles provide the base of support for the entire squat. During the descent, the ankles move into dorsiflexion in the sagittal plane, allowing knees to track forward over toes. Tibialis anterior controls this dorsiflexion; peroneals maintain lateral stability and prevent inversion. The gastrocnemius and soleus work eccentrically on the way down and concentrically on the way up, keeping the heel grounded and weight evenly distributed.

## Knees

**Muscles involved:** Quadriceps (rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, vastus intermedius), hamstrings (biceps femoris, semitendinosus, semimembranosus), gastrocnemius.

**Function:** The knee flexes during descent and extends during ascent (sagittal plane). Quadriceps work eccentrically on the way down, concentrically on the way up — the primary agonist. Hamstrings act as synergists and stabilise the knee, preventing excessive tibial forward translation. Proper knee tracking over the second toe is essential for minimising joint stress.

## Hips

**Muscles involved:** Gluteus maximus (primary agonist), hamstrings (synergists), gluteus medius/minimus (stabilisers), hip flexors/iliopsoas (antagonists during hip extension).

**Function:** The hips are the primary power driver. On descent, hips flex (sagittal plane) while glute max works eccentrically. On ascent, glute max contracts concentrically to produce hip extension — returning to standing. Gluteus medius stabilises the pelvis in the frontal plane, preventing hip drop or valgus knee collapse.

## Spine

**Muscles involved:** Erector spinae, multifidus, quadratus lumborum, transversus abdominis, rectus abdominis.

**Function:** Spine must maintain neutral position — preserving its S-curve — throughout the squat. Excessive flexion stresses intervertebral discs. Erector spinae and multifidus work isometrically to resist spinal flexion. Transversus abdominis creates intra-abdominal pressure stabilising the lumbar spine. This coordinated isometric "core canister" contraction is a textbook example of stabiliser muscles protecting the kinetic chain.

## Shoulders

**Muscles involved:** Trapezius (upper, middle, lower), deltoids, rhomboids, rotator cuff (infraspinatus, supraspinatus, subscapularis, teres minor).

**Function:** While not primary movers, the shoulders play a critical stabilising role that directly influences squat quality and safety. The trapezius (middle/lower fibres) and rhomboids maintain scapular retraction and depression, keeping chest open and thoracic spine extended — preventing upper back rounding that would compromise neutral spine.

The rotator cuff stabilises the glenohumeral joint, preventing unwanted movement. This is particularly important in a loaded back squat, where the barbell rests across upper traps. Shoulders must maintain external rotation and retraction to create a stable shelf — reducing risk of the weight shifting forward and increasing lumbar load.

In a bodyweight squat, arms are typically in shoulder flexion (sagittal plane), acting as a counterbalance to maintain centre of mass as hips descend. The anterior deltoid maintains this isometrically.

From a kinetic chain perspective, any breakdown at the shoulder — such as protracted scapulae or internally rotated humeri — creates a ripple effect downward, often contributing to thoracic flexion and increased lumbar stress. This perfectly illustrates how dysfunction in one kinetic chain component directly affects all others throughout a movement.

## Why This Matters for Teaching Pilates

### 1. Breathing Has Real Physiology Behind It

- Diaphragm + intercostals are primary respiratory muscles
- Proper breathing = better O₂ delivery + thoracic mobility + core stability
- Respiratory pump improves venous return (circulation back to heart)
- Poor breathing patterns → restricted spine/ribs, anxiety, fatigue
- Teaching breath patterns = genuine physiological benefit for clients

### 2. Cueing Works Through the Nervous System

- Every verbal cue = input to CNS shaping motor output
- Clear, simple cues respect the three stages of motor learning
- Over-cueing clients in cognitive stage = information overload
- Autonomous clients need progression challenges
- Tactile cues can bypass cognitive processing when verbal cues fail

### 3. Kinetic Chain = Foundation of Posture Assessment

- Dysfunction at one joint causes cascading compensations
- Collapsed arch → hip drop → shoulder tension
- This is WHY Pilates assesses whole-body posture, not isolated areas
- Helps identify root cause, not just symptoms

### 4. Train All Three Planes of Motion

- Most daily movement is sagittal (forward-back)
- Programs must include frontal and transverse work
- Prevents imbalances and real-world injury patterns
- Classical Pilates repertoire naturally includes this

### 5. Full Muscle Action Spectrum

- Eccentric and isometric = critical for joint health
- Pilates naturally emphasises this (slow, controlled, held positions)
- Reformer springs deliver resistance in all three phases
- This is one of Pilates' key advantages over traditional weight training

### 6. Proprioception Is Trainable

- Single-leg work, balance challenges, unfamiliar patterns
- Train the nervous system, not just muscles
- Explains why Pilates improves balance in older adults
- Mat work on unstable surfaces deepens proprioceptive challenge

### 7. Rest Intervals Shape Adaptation

- Understanding energy systems = knowing why to rest 30 sec vs 3 min
- Classical flows (minimal rest) train glycolytic + oxidative
- Heavy spring work with rest trains ATP-PCr
- Programming choice affects which system adapts

### 8. Muscle Spindle & GTO Knowledge = Better Stretching

- Held stretches activate GTO → muscle relaxes deeper
- Explains why slow, sustained stretches work better than bouncing
- This is the science behind classical stretch cues

### 9. Stabiliser Muscles Matter Most

- Deep muscles (TVA, multifidus, rotator cuff) = unsung heroes
- Pilates emphasises these from Day 1
- Feed-forward activation trained with precise cueing
- Sets Pilates apart from pure cardio or weight training

### 10. Bone Health Through Weight-Bearing

- Reformer work IS weight-bearing (against springs)
- Mat Pilates = weight-bearing through gravity
- Especially important for female clients 40+
- Safer than high-impact but delivers stimulus
